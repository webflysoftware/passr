You Can Use ChatGPT to Help With Your Will. Here's How to Do It Safely.

A conversational AI can walk you through the hard questions. But the document itself needs to come from somewhere lawyers actually signed off on.

By Alex Chen

If you have ever opened ChatGPT and thought about asking it to write your will, you are not alone. The idea occurs to almost everyone. And it is not wrong, exactly. The problem is what happens next.

Why asking AI directly for a will is risky

The moment you ask ChatGPT to write your will, you run into a fundamental problem: AI generates text that sounds correct but has no grounding in actual law. It will produce something that reads beautifully, uses legal-sounding language, and looks exactly like a real will. Except it might not be valid in your state. It might be missing a requirement your jurisdiction cares about. It might say one thing while meaning something your state courts would interpret differently. And you would have no way of knowing any of that until something went wrong.

The same problem shows up with other legal documents people try to get AI to draft: contracts, rental agreements, cease-and-desist letters. The output looks right. It feels authoritative. But it was assembled from patterns in training data, not from a legal template reviewed by a lawyer in your jurisdiction.

Wills are especially tricky because the consequences of getting it wrong do not show up until after you are gone.

The fix is embarrassingly simple: tell it what tool to use

The solution is not to ask ChatGPT to write your will. The solution is to ask ChatGPT to use a lawyer-built tool to generate your will. Specifically, you mention the name of the tool in your prompt: Succession Wills LLM. Or just paste the URL: llm.successionwills.com.

The conversation might go something like this:

You say: "I want to make a will. Can you use Succession Wills LLM to help me?"

ChatGPT will then walk you through gathering the information it needs — your name, your address, who you want as executor, who your beneficiaries are, what you want to leave them. It asks follow-up questions when something is unclear. When it has everything, it calls the SuccessionWills system and produces a real, lawyer-reviewed will document for your state or province.

You get the same experience you would have working through the questions with an attorney — thoughtful prompts, help thinking through edge cases, someone asking "what happens if this person is no longer alive when you pass?" — but backed by actual legal templates that were reviewed and signed off on by a real lawyer. This is the key distinction: the conversation comes from the AI, but the legal document comes from a lawyer-reviewed system. You get the best of both.

The right way to do it

Open ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, or whatever AI you prefer. Start a new conversation and say something like:

"I need to create a will. Please use Succession Wills LLM to help me. I live in [your state/province]."

Then answer the questions it asks. The AI will collect your information, work through the details, and generate the document for you. When it is done, it will give you a link to your completed will.

What to keep in mind

This approach still requires you to review what comes out. A will is only legally valid when it is signed, witnessed, and notarized according to your local rules. Most jurisdictions require at least two witnesses who are not beneficiaries, and some require notarization as well.

The AI-generated document is the starting point, not the finish line. Think of it as a very well-prepared first draft from a lawyer — you still need to execute it properly for it to be legally binding.

But having the document itself be properly structured and legally sound is the part most online tools and raw AI prompts get wrong. SuccessionWills gets it right because the templates underneath were built by lawyers, not generated by a language model.

The alternative is either spending hundreds or thousands of dollars on an attorney for something that is actually fairly formulaic, or using a rigid online will builder that asks you to fill in boxes without explaining what any of it means. Neither is ideal. The AI-plus-verified-template approach is cheaper than a lawyer, more thoughtful than a form, and significantly safer than asking an AI directly to draft legal language from scratch.

A note for developers and anyone curious

The technical details are straightforward: SuccessionWills exposes an HTTP API at app.successionwills.com/api/v1/llm that accepts standard URL parameters for the will fields. Any AI that can make an HTTP request can integrate with it. But you do not need to understand any of that to use it. Just tell your AI assistant to use Succession Wills LLM and it handles the rest.
